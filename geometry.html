What an image cannot tell you

Relative depth, physical scale, and ambiguous surfaces

A single image does not uniquely determine scene scale. In the pinhole camera model, projection depends on X over Z and Y over Z. Scaling every coordinate by the same positive factor leaves these ratios unchanged. Learned cues such as occlusion, perspective, and familiar objects help a model infer plausible geometry, but they do not make the answer unique. Relative depth describes near and far structure. Relative inverse depth is a proximity-like value; higher means closer in our common comparison convention. Metric depth has physical units. These quantities must stay separate. The viewer colors each map using its own range, so matching colors across maps do not imply matching distances. Mirrors and glass introduce another question: are we describing the physical surface or the scene visible through reflection or transmission?

A model can infer plausible geometry without recovering a uniquely determined physical scene. That distinction starts with the camera.

The scale ambiguity

In the pinhole model, a scene point projects to image coordinates through u = fₓX/Z + cₓ and v = fᵧY/Z + cᵧ. Here X and Y locate the point laterally, Z is distance along the camera’s optical axis, f is focal length in pixels, and c is the principal point. Multiply X, Y, and Z by the same positive number: both ratios stay unchanged.

A tiny scene close to the camera and a proportionally larger scene farther away can therefore share the same projection. Recognizable objects, perspective, occlusion, texture gradients, and learned scene statistics constrain plausible answers. These are useful priors, but they do not remove every ambiguity. Even a familiar chair can have an unusual size.

Three quantities that must stay separate

QuantityMeaningWhat it supports
Relative depthNear/far structure in an arbitrary scale; some systems also permit a shift.Ordering and shape comparisons under an explicit convention.
Relative inverse depth qA proximity-like output; for the V2 and AC checkpoints, higher means closer.Within-image comparisons. A raw value of 2 is not two meters.
Metric depth ZDistance in physical units under a specified camera/depth convention.Distance error against compatible reference measurements.

The DA3 checkpoint used here produces relative depth with the opposite direction: smaller means closer. Our comparison converts positive DA3 depth to inverse depth before comparing annotated points. This harmonizes direction; it does not calibrate scale or make raw values comparable across models.

Do not confuse camera-axis Z with Euclidean range along a ray. Away from the principal point, the latter is longer. A dataset, a renderer, and a reconstruction tool must agree on this convention before a meter-valued comparison is meaningful.

Color is a display choice

The experiment viewer normalizes each prediction by its own minimum and maximum and applies a color ramp. Bright yellow indicates larger inverse-depth values within that map. Two equally yellow pixels in different maps need not have the same raw value, much less the same physical distance. Compare ordering and recorded metrics, not color alone.

Why boundaries are difficult

A pixel near an object edge can combine foreground and background appearance. A chair leg may occupy fewer pixels than the model’s internal representation preserves. A mirror shows a virtual scene behind a nearby reflective surface. Glass combines transmitted background, reflections, and the object’s own contours. Before declaring an error, specify the intended surface: physical glass, visible background, or reflected content.

Early neural depth work by Eigen, Puhrsch, and Fergus combined coarse scene structure with local refinement [1]. The lesson that global context and local detail both matter remains useful even when the network architecture changes.

Try this reasoning exercise

Imagine doubling the size and distance of every object in a scene while keeping the camera unchanged. Explain why the image can stay the same. Then name one extra source of evidence that could constrain scale: a known object dimension, calibrated camera information with a suitable model, a depth sensor, or additional motion/view geometry.
