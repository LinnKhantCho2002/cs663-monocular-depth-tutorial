Depth, scale, and the camera

Understanding what the numbers in a depth map mean

Think about a chair in front of a wall. A photograph often gives enough clues to order them, but their physical distances are less certain. The camera equation explains part of the problem: projection depends on X divided by Z and Y divided by Z. Doubling every scene coordinate leaves those ratios unchanged. Relative depth captures near and far structure on an arbitrary scale. In the inverse-depth convention used here, a larger value means closer. Metric depth reports physical distance with a defined camera convention. The color display normalizes each map separately, so equal colors across models do not imply equal distances. Glass and mirrors add a surface question: are we estimating the nearby material or the scene visible through it? Keep that question in mind when looking at the marked points.

You can usually tell that a chair is in front of a wall. Working out how many meters separate them is a harder problem. A single photograph provides clues about depth, but leaves the scale of the scene uncertain.

Why scale is ambiguous

Imagine photographing a small model room. If every object and its distance from the camera were doubled, the new scene could produce the same image. The objects would be larger, but they would also be farther away.

The pinhole camera equation shows why. A point at coordinates X, Y, Z projects to u = fₓX/Z + cₓ and v = fᵧY/Z + cᵧ. Here Z is depth along the camera’s optical axis, fₓ and fᵧ are focal lengths in pixels, and cₓ and cᵧ locate the principal point. Multiply X, Y, and Z by the same positive factor and both ratios stay the same.

A depth model works with visual clues that help narrow down the possibilities. Overlapping objects give local ordering. Perspective and texture gradients suggest how a floor recedes. Familiar objects provide approximate size expectations. These clues are often useful together, although an unusually large chair or an unfamiliar camera can upset the assumptions learned during training.

Suppose a relative inverse-depth map gives one point a value of 2 and another a value of 4. Under our convention, the second point is closer. The arbitrary scale and possible shift prevent us from reading those values as meters or concluding that one distance is exactly half the other.

V2 and AC already use the higher-means-closer direction. Our DA3 checkpoint produces relative depth, where smaller values mean closer, so the comparison converts its positive output to inverse depth. Each model still has its own numerical scale.

Metric depth also needs a definition of distance. Camera-axis depth Z measures distance along the optical axis. Euclidean range measures distance along the viewing ray. These differ away from the center of the image, so a renderer, dataset, and reconstruction program need compatible conventions.

Reading the colors

The viewer maps each prediction’s minimum and maximum to a color ramp. In the inverse-depth display, yellow marks larger values and dark purple marks smaller ones. This makes the layout easier to see, but removes the numerical scale from the colors.

Two yellow regions in different maps can therefore represent different raw values. To compare models, use the point ordering or a defined error measure. Color alone can make maps look more similar than their predictions are.

Glass, mirrors, and thin edges

For an ordinary opaque object, the visible surface is often a clear target. A mirror complicates this: its physical surface is nearby, while its reflection depicts a different scene. Glass can show the background and its own reflections at once. An annotation has to specify which surface is being compared.

Thin objects create a different difficulty. A chair leg may occupy only a few image pixels, and pixels along its edge can mix foreground and background appearance. Losing that detail inside a network makes it difficult for the final output to recover a sharp boundary.

Early work by Eigen, Puhrsch, and Fergus combined a coarse prediction of scene layout with local refinement [1]. Modern models use different components, but still face the need to combine overall layout with fine boundaries.

Check your understanding

If a model orders a sofa and a wall correctly, what else would you need to estimate the sofa’s physical distance? Think about known dimensions, camera information with a suitable model, a sensor measurement, or additional views.
