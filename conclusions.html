The strongest conclusion is specific: these checkpoints behave differently on these inputs. Research depth comes from explaining the boundaries of that observation.

What this project establishes

We implemented real single-image inference, retained every prediction in a fixed three-model comparison, and examined two different types of reference. V2 and AC agree in aggregate on clean ordering, with modest differences under synthetic degradation. DA3-Small behaves differently on the same one-view inputs and resolves one clean mirror annotation that the other two miss. A newer model therefore need not improve every tested condition, and one aggregate need not describe every scene.

Six NYU examples let us inspect sensor support and shape agreement after oracle alignment. Their small aligned errors should not be read as evidence that a relative checkpoint can report physical distances without calibration. The browser activities make these distinctions visible: pair accuracy, approximate probes, reference depth, and an alignment fit appear as separate quantities.

Applications determine the acceptable mistake

For photographic effects, plausible foreground/background ordering may be enough to guide a blur mask, but missing a glass boundary can produce a visible artifact. For scene understanding, depth can complement RGB features, yet an unknown scale limits metric reasoning. For robotics or reconstruction, physical dimensions, calibration, temporal consistency, and failure detection become central. Our static examples do not validate operation in those systems.

For an edge-device project, model size is one constraint rather than a complete deployment result. Our warmed laptop medians were approximately 0.123, 0.170, and 0.412 seconds for V2, AC, and DA3 on one indoor input. These measurements exclude loading and plotting and use a specific backend. Phone latency, energy, memory peaks, thermal behavior, and camera-stream performance remain unmeasured. DepthART motivates investigating compression; it supplies no measurement for our hardware.

What I would test next

  1. Camera-specific generalization: acquire a separate, documented set of photographs and measured or independently annotated targets. Split before tuning and audit training overlap where possible.
  2. Physical degradation: capture controlled exposure changes and motion with a fixed scene rather than relying only on postprocessing. Preserve camera metadata and distinguish missing evidence from model instability.
  3. Adaptation: compare a frozen baseline with a controlled fine-tuning run, evaluate clean-domain retention, and ablate the adaptation objective. Checkpoint comparisons alone cannot isolate causes.
  4. Geometry and deployment: select a metric variant or multi-view task deliberately, define the output convention, and evaluate on the actual target camera and device.

Questions to answer in your own words

Why does image scale ambiguity survive a convincing prediction? What does a pretrained encoder contribute? Which parameters or objective change during adaptation? Why can an oracle-aligned metric improve without better uncalibrated distances? What evidence would distinguish physical robustness from stability under digital dimming?

If you can explain those points using a concrete scene and a named protocol, you understand more than how to operate a demo. The next step is to turn that explanation into your own spoken presentation, using the tutorial to show both a success and a failure.

Project boundaries

Local checkpoint inference and evaluation were executed. No new model was trained; no full published benchmark was reproduced; no phone deployment was tested. Marigold, Metric3Dv2, Depth Pro, and DepthART are literature coverage. The full presentation video and course submissions are separate deliverables.
