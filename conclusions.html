The three models agree on much of the clean-image ordering, but their results separate under dimming and blur. The mirror example also shows why it helps to inspect individual cases alongside the totals.

The main findings

V2 and AC each predict 34 of 35 clean pairs correctly. Under strong digital dimming, AC keeps 34 correct while V2 keeps 32. Under blur, the counts are 33 and 32. These are modest differences on the selected images, with the adapted AC checkpoint retaining more correct pairs in those conditions.

DA3-Small gets 33 clean pairs correct, dropping to 25 under strong dimming and 24 under blur. On the clean mirror example, however, it correctly orders the pair that V2 and AC miss. Choosing a model from an aggregate count alone would hide that case.

The NYU examples add a view of dense shape agreement. V2 and AC have similar mean aligned errors on these six scenes, while DA3’s mean is higher. Because the alignment uses the reference, these scores describe how the shapes compare after fitting scale and shift. The evaluation page explains the protocol and its limits.

How the application changes the choice

For a photographic blur effect, foreground/background ordering can help create a mask. A missing glass rim or chair leg would show up as an editing artifact, so boundary quality matters even when the broad layout looks right.

For reconstruction or robotics, physical dimensions and consistency across views or time become more important. The model choice needs to match the camera, output units, and available measurements. The metric and any-view papers offer approaches to investigate for those requirements.

On a resource-limited device, accuracy also has to be weighed against memory and latency. Our warmed laptop medians on one indoor image were about 0.123 seconds for V2, 0.170 for AC, and 0.412 for DA3. A phone or camera stream would require its own measurements, including loading, memory peaks, energy use, and sustained performance. DepthART provides a recent starting point for studying smaller models.

Experiments I would add

The next step would be a separate camera-specific test set with measured depth or independently annotated ordering. Splitting the data before tuning would make it possible to check generalization without choosing parameters on the same examples used for the final score.

I would also compare digitally dimmed images with photographs captured at different exposures. A fixed scene and recorded camera settings would help separate changes in visibility from effects introduced by image postprocessing.

For fine-tuning, I would keep an untouched pretrained baseline and evaluate both difficult inputs and clean scenes. A controlled ablation could then test the consistency objective or spatial constraint while holding the rest of the training setup fixed.

Review the ideas with an example

Return to one scene in the explorer and explain the output in your own words. Which surface is being estimated? Are the values relative or metric? What does the annotation verify? If the prediction changes under dimming, which score captures that change?

Those questions connect the camera geometry, model design, and evaluation to the actual result. They are also a good way to decide what evidence another experiment should collect.

Scope of the project

This project ran three pretrained checkpoints and a small fixed evaluation. Training a new model, reproducing a full paper benchmark, and testing a phone deployment remain future work.
