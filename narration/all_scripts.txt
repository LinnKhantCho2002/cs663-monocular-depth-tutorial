Depth from one image
====================
This tutorial is about estimating depth from one photo. When we look at a room, we can usually tell which objects are in front and which are farther away. But the photo doesn't give us a distance measurement. How does a model work that out, and when does it get confused? I compared three released models using photos, dimming, and blur, plus a few indoor scenes with sensor depth. We'll start with a mirror photo, then work through the geometry, models, and results. Try the questions as you go. You don't need to install anything to use the examples.


Which point is closer?
======================
Take a look at the mirror photo first. Which point would you put closer to the camera, A or B? Choose an answer before opening the model results. I used this photo because a reflection makes the depth target less obvious. For this project, I ran three pretrained models on fifteen photographs, then tried dimming and blur. I also used six indoor scenes with sensor depth. We'll use those examples to look at what the models get right and where they struggle. The short questions are ungraded. Make a choice, read the explanation, and try another answer if you want.


Depth, scale, and the camera
============================
If I make a room twice as big and move everything twice as far from the camera, could the photo look the same? Try the first question before reading the equation. This is why estimating near and far is easier than recovering meters from one image. In our inverse-depth maps, a larger value means closer, but the numbers have an arbitrary scale. The colors question is worth trying too. Each map uses its own range, so matching colors can be misleading. With glass or a mirror, we also have to ask which surface the prediction describes.


How the models learn depth
==========================
Look at the room before and after dimming. Suppose a model gives the same answer in both. Does that make it right? That's the first question here. A model can be stable and still repeat a mistake. The encoder and decoder sections explain how image features become depth predictions. V2 learns from a teacher; Marigold adapts a diffusion model; AC uses consistency to adapt a pretrained depth model. Try the scale and offset controls farther down, then move the third point. You'll see why changing a value's scale is different from changing the shape. I used released checkpoints, not a model I trained.


Putting the predictions to a test
=================================
Before reading the results, try the question about fitting a prediction to sensor depth. The distinction matters because the reference helps us choose the scale and shift. A low error after that fit tells us about shape agreement; it doesn't mean the model knew those distances on its own. I used two checks: annotated near and far pairs, and six NYU sensor-depth examples. The code and table below show how they were scored. This is a small comparison, so I wouldn't use it to declare one model best everywhere. You can also download a saved case and check the calculation yourself.

Start with the original mirror photo and pair two. Switch models and compare their answers, then reveal the depth maps. After that, keep the model fixed and change the input. You could try the tripod room under strong dimming or inspect a glass rim. Move A or B to look at another location, then try the question about what the annotation still covers. Pick one mismatch and describe it before relying on the overall number.


What the comparison showed
==========================
The mirror example is useful, but is one correct pair enough to choose a model? Try the question first. In this sample, AC kept more pairs correct under dimming and blur. DA3 got a mirror pair right that the other two missed. Which result matters depends on the application. A blur effect needs clean edges. Reconstruction needs suitable distances. A small device needs acceptable speed and memory use. My next test would use a separate camera dataset and real low-light photos. For now, choose one example and explain what was measured and what you'd still want to check.
