Testing monocular depth models
==============================
This tutorial compares three recent models that predict depth from a single photo: Depth Anything V2, DepthAnything-AC, and Depth Anything 3. I ran their Small versions on the same fifteen photos, then repeated the tests with dimming and blur. AC is adapted from V2, so I wanted to compare the two on darker and blurred photos. The guides explain the methods and show the results through a mirror, a traffic scene, and a kitchen. I ran the released checkpoints for this project.


Which point is closer?
======================
A mirror shows a nearby surface and a reflection of another scene, which makes depth harder to judge. A and B mark two points in this photo. Choose which looks closer before revealing the answers. The models disagree about their ordering. The maps below show the values behind each answer: a larger inverse-depth value means closer. The dataset label gives us an answer to check against, and the table shows what each model predicted at those same positions.


Depth, scale, and the camera
============================
The room diagram shows why physical scale is difficult to recover from one photo. Double the size of every object and its distance from the camera, and the image can stay the same. Depth models use cues such as overlap, perspective, and familiar objects to estimate which surfaces are closer. The models tested here produce relative values. In the maps, yellow marks the closer end of each prediction's color range. Each map uses its own minimum and maximum, so the colors need to be read within that map.


How the models learn depth
==========================
These three models use different training approaches. V2 starts with pretrained image features and learns depth from labels generated by another model, called a teacher. AC's authors adapt V2 to preserve predictions through appearance changes. DA3 is designed to work with one or several views. In this experiment, I gave it one image at a time. Comparing the original and changed predictions shows how stable the answers are. Comparing those answers with the dataset labels shows which ones are correct. The three-point example below explains how normalization removes differences in scale and offset.


Putting the predictions to a test
=================================
I used two types of reference. DA-2K labels which of two points is closer. NYU provides indoor sensor depth, so I can compare surfaces across the image. For NYU, I fitted each prediction's scale and shift using the sensor reference, then calculated the error. In the kitchen, V2's mean absolute relative error is about two point six percent after alignment. The average is low, but the error image shows a mismatch near the counter edge. The results table brings together the point-pair counts, aligned errors, and inference times.


Test what changes
=================
This page lets you compare the same photo under dimming and blur. The original photo stays visible while you change the input condition. Each choice loads the model output saved for that input. In the traffic example, V2 gets all three pairs right on the original, then one right under severe dimming. AC keeps all three right. You can switch models to compare their predictions on the same photo. The optional point inspector reads other locations in the saved map. The kitchen controls switch between the prediction, sensor reference, and error image.


What the comparison showed
==========================
Across the fifteen photos, V2 and AC both get thirty-four of thirty-five pairs right on the originals. With strong dimming, AC keeps thirty-four correct and V2 falls to thirty-two. With blur, AC gets thirty-three right and V2 gets thirty-two. DA3 answers the mirror comparison correctly, while its overall count falls further under dimming and blur. I would next test photos captured at different exposures to compare real lighting changes with digital dimming.
