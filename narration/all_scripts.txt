Monocular depth estimation
==========================
Start with the car mirror. Two models reverse an annotated near and far pair, while a third gets it right. The maps look plausible, so checking the points reveals something we might otherwise miss. For this project, I compared V2 Small, AC Small, and DA3 Small on fifteen photographs, including dimmed and blurred versions. I also checked six indoor scenes against sensor depth. The tutorial explains what their values mean, how recent models learn depth, and how I scored the results. Then you can inspect the predictions yourself. The website displays saved runs. The Python walkthrough lets you run a new photograph.


Depth, scale, and the camera
============================
A chair can be in front of a wall without us knowing its distance in meters. If we double a scene's size and its distance from the camera, the same image can result. That is the scale ambiguity. Relative depth estimates near and far on an arbitrary scale. In the inverse-depth maps here, larger values mean closer. The colors are normalized separately, so yellow in two maps does not mean the same physical distance. Mirrors raise another question: are we estimating the nearby mirror surface or the scene in its reflection? Think about which surface an annotation describes before treating the model's answer as correct.


How the models learn depth
==========================
The encoder extracts image features, and the decoder turns them into a depth map. Early systems combined a coarse estimate with local refinement. Transformer models let image patches exchange information before combining features across resolutions. V2 learns from a teacher's depth labels. Marigold adapts a diffusion model. AC fine-tunes a depth model to preserve geometry through appearance changes, with a frozen model guiding it. Open the AC example and change scale and offset, then move one point: matching the overall scale cannot fix every shape error. I ran the V2, AC, and DA3 checkpoints. The other models are research comparisons, and I did not train a new model.


Putting the predictions to a test
=================================
The point-pair test asks which marked point is closer. V2 and AC each get thirty-four of thirty-five clean pairs right. AC retains more correct pairs under strong dimming and blur. The NYU test compares dense predictions with sensor depth after fitting scale and shift. The fit uses the same reference pixels as the score, so a low error shows aligned shape agreement, not raw accuracy in meters. This is a small fixed sample, and transformed versions are not independent scenes. You can download the results, recompute a saved kitchen example, or use the expanded walkthrough to run a new photo.


Explore the depth maps
======================
Choose pair two in the car mirror and keep the original input. Switch between V2, AC, and DA3, then compare each answer with the annotation. Reveal the map and adjust the overlay to inspect the reflection. Next, try strong dimming in the tripod room or magnify a glass rim. You can move A and B to inspect any region, but those new positions have no reference labels. The profile follows an image line, not a physical distance through the scene. In the NYU explorer, compare the aligned prediction with the reference and error map. Look for a specific mismatch that the overall score may hide.


What the comparison showed
==========================
AC preserves more correct point pairs under dimming and blur in this sample. DA3 gets a mirror pair right that V2 and AC miss. Neither observation settles which model is best for every application. A blur effect needs good boundaries; reconstruction needs suitable physical distances; a small device also needs acceptable memory use and latency. My next tests would use a separate camera-specific dataset and actual low-light photographs. For fine-tuning, I would compare with an untouched baseline on both difficult and clean scenes. Return to one example and explain its surface, output units, and reference. That is a useful way to check what the result supports.
