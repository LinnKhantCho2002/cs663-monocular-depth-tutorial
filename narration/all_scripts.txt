Testing monocular depth models
==============================
This project asks how recent monocular depth models behave when an image becomes difficult. These models predict depth from one photograph. I compared V2 Small, AC Small, and DA3 Small on the same fifteen photos, then repeated the test with dimming and blur. My main question is which models keep the near and far ordering correct, and how the adapted AC checkpoint compares with V2. The paper review explains foundation models and adaptation. The mirror, traffic, and kitchen examples let us examine the evidence. Geometry provides the background. I used released checkpoints, and my contribution is the comparison and analysis.


Which point is closer?
======================
Look at the two points in the mirror photo. Which would you place closer to the camera? Make your choice before opening the explanation or the three model maps. A reflection is useful here because what we see isn't as simple as an opaque object in front of a wall. Once you reveal the results, compare each model's value at A with its value at B. A larger inverse-depth value means closer in this tutorial. The source annotation checks these two points. It doesn't tell us whether every part of the map is right. Keep that distinction in mind as we move on.


Depth, scale, and the camera
============================
Suppose we build a room twice as big and move everything twice as far from the camera. Could the photograph stay the same? The first question shows why a single image leaves the physical scale uncertain. A model can still predict near and far using what it learned about images. But a relative depth value isn't a distance in meters. The colors can be confusing too: each model map gets its own color range. Try the second question before comparing yellow patches across maps. For a mirror or glass, we also need to agree on which surface we're trying to measure.


How the models learn depth
==========================
The model names can make this sound complicated, so start with the three questions in the table. V2 uses pretrained image features and learns depth from a teacher's labels. AC adapts a V2 model to preserve its predictions when appearance changes. DA3 takes a different approach to geometry, and I tested its Small checkpoint with one image. Look at the traffic photo before and after dimming, then reveal the saved results. The scene hasn't moved, so a changed near/far answer matters. Consistency still isn't proof of accuracy. The reference labels let us check that separately. I used these checkpoints; I didn't fine-tune them myself.


Putting the predictions to a test
=================================
The point labels checked which of two locations was closer. In the kitchen, we have a sensor reference to compare across many pixels. There's a catch: these model outputs use relative scales, so I fit scale and shift using that reference. The low error describes agreement after the fit. It doesn't show that the model knew the distances in meters on its own. Look at the outlined counter edge in the kitchen images. The error map helps you find a local mismatch that the average number hides. Then read the full results table to see how these examples fit into the wider sample.

What the comparison showed
==========================
Across this sample, AC retains more correct point pairs than V2 under strong dimming and blur, while their original-input counts match. That answers the main comparison question, but it doesn't tell us which training component caused the difference. DA3 gets the mirror pair right and keeps all three traffic pairs correct, yet its overall count drops more under the difficult inputs. The kitchen score measures shape after fitting to the sensor reference, not independent meter accuracy. My contribution is testing these released checkpoints under the same conditions and interpreting those results. A larger separate test set and real low-light capture would be the next checks.
