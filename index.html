In the car-mirror example in this project, two models get an annotated near/far pair wrong. A third gets it right. Looking at the full depth maps, it is easy to miss that difference.

That example is a useful starting point for monocular depth estimation: predicting scene depth from a single RGB image. A model can produce a convincing map of a room or a street, yet still struggle with the surfaces we most want to inspect—glass, reflections, thin objects, or details lost in poor lighting.

For this project, I compared three pretrained models: Depth Anything V2 Small, DepthAnything-AC Small, and Depth Anything 3 Small. I ran them on the same photographs, changed the brightness and blur, and checked their predictions against annotated point pairs. I also used six indoor scenes with sensor depth to examine how well their predicted shapes matched a reference.

The tutorial explains the ideas behind those experiments. We will start with why a single image leaves depth ambiguous, then look at how foundation models are adapted for depth prediction. The second half covers the evaluation and lets you explore the results directly.

What you will learn

  • How relative depth differs from a distance measured in meters.
  • What an encoder and a dense prediction decoder do.
  • How pretraining, pseudo-labeling, and fine-tuning appear in recent depth models.
  • How to read a depth map and check a prediction against a reference.
  • What the results suggest about robustness, model choice, and future experiments.

Before you start

You will need basic familiarity with images and neural networks. The geometry section introduces the camera equation, and the evaluation section walks through a short NumPy example. You can follow the main discussion without running the code.

Try an example first

If you prefer to start with the demo, open Experiments. Choose the car mirror, keep the original input, and select pair 2. Switch between the three models and compare the marked points. Then return to the geometry section to see why the meaning of “depth” matters for a reflective surface.

The website loads predictions saved from local Python runs. You can compare models, inspect points, and change the display in your browser. Fresh inference on a new photograph is available in the accompanying local implementation.
